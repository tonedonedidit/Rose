# Contractor partnerships: how successful firms do it, and what to decide

Research done 2026-10-14. Sources are linked. Where a source was thin or unverified, it says so.

## What the research found

**1. Sell the design as its own product (Yardzen).**
Yardzen charges homeowners a flat fee for a remote 3D design package, then connects them with a vetted local contractor to build it. Its own site cites design pricing from $495 to $1,495. How it is paid by contractors is not confirmed in any source I found.
Takeaway for Stone & Rose: the 3D design is the product, and a "3D Render Only" tier lets someone buy it without committing to a build. Sources: [Yardzen FAQ](https://www.yardzen.com/faq), [Dealroom profile](https://app.dealroom.co/companies/yardzen).

**2. Pool builders already sell with 3D.**
Pool builders commonly use 3D design software, and a common workflow is the builder sharing the design file so a landscape contractor adds plants, lighting and furniture. Structure Studios (maker of Pool Studio) grew by serving pool builders first, then landscape and hardscape contractors. I found no formal "white-label renders for pool builders" program from a landscape firm, so that is an open lane, and also a sign the market may not know to ask for it yet.
Sources: [Landscape Architect: 3D pool design](https://landscapearchitect.com/landscape-articles/3d-pool-design), [Pool & Spa Marketing: 3D design and profit](https://poolspamarketing.com/trade/using-3-d-design-software-to-increase-profits-and-client-satisfaction/print), [Lawn & Landscape: Structure Studios](https://www.lawnandlandscape.com/news/ll-033012-structure-studios-10-years).

**3. Builders and architects want reliability, early involvement and a written arrangement.**
Trade guidance says builders value a finished punch list on schedule over a low bid, architects want their designs built as drawn, and referrals work both ways. Unilock's contractor guide recommends a written referral agreement, even a simple email one. A Twin Cities design-build firm that gets much of its work from builders stresses early coordination with grading, drainage and utilities. Paying design partners promptly strengthens the relationship.
Sources: [Unilock Contractors](https://contractor.unilock.com/?p=63306), [Leanscaper partnerships guide](https://leanscaper.com/blog/how-to-build-landscaping-partnerships-that-scale-your-business), [NRD Landscape: partner for builders](https://nrdlandscape.com/for-builders/), [Lawn & Landscape: Tecza](https://www.lawnandlandscape.com/For-The-Love-Of-Design--Quality-Control--Tecza-Environmental-Group.aspx).
One claim in the Unilock piece (formal referral programs grow faster) traces to a secondhand citation. Do not quote it.

**4. Three ways firms charge for design.**
Free design, a design fee credited toward the build, or a non-refundable fee. Design-build contractors tend to credit or waive the fee to win construction; standalone designers rely on it. The risk of free design is the client taking your plans to a cheaper installer; a fee also screens out price shoppers. Examples: Wayside rebates the design fee up to 5% of the project price if the work is done within 12 months; one firm reports a design fee of about 10% of the approved construction budget and credits it against later invoices. No source gave an industry conversion rate from paid design to build.
Sources: [NALP: charging design fees](https://blog.landscapeprofessionals.org/design-build-opting-to-charge-design-fees/), [Structure Studios: how to charge](https://www.structurestudios.com/blog/whats-the-best-way-to-charge-for-a-landscape-design), [Lawn & Landscape: Wayside](https://lawnandlandscape.com/article/how-we-do-it--design-fees---rebates-at-wayside).

## Decisions only Tone can make (my recommendation first)

| Decision | Recommendation | Why |
|---|---|---|
| How a contractor pays for 3D design | Per-project design fee, with the fee credited if the contractor's client also hires Stone & Rose for any build work | Keeps design valued, filters price shoppers, matches how design-build firms charge |
| Do you build, or only design? | Decide and state it on the site | Contractors will ask first. The old site never said |
| Referral payments to contractors | Hold until the first 3 partners exist; start with a written one-page agreement | Avoids promising terms you have not tested |
| Pilot | One pool builder, one real project, renders plus walk-through video, then a case study with their permission | Gives proof for outreach |
| Turnaround time | Measure it on the first 3 projects, then publish a number | A guessed number becomes a promise |

## Gaps in the current research and site (what you may not know)

1. **Service area is unconfirmed.** The new site lists nine Phoenix-area cities as a default. Search engines and AI assistants repeat what the site says, so a wrong city list sends the wrong leads. Confirm it.
2. **No license number on the site.** Arizona contractors can look up a license on the Registrar of Contractors site. If Stone & Rose is licensed, show the number. If design is done without a contractor license, that is a different question to ask the ROC. This is a trust gap and possibly a compliance question, not just marketing.
3. **No reviews.** For local searches, Google Business Profile and reviews often matter more than the website. Zero visible reviews is the biggest findability gap after the profile itself.
4. **Google Business Profile is not part of this site.** It is a separate listing you control at google.com/business. A service-area business can hide its street address. It needs the same name, phone and email as the site.
5. **Analytics and Search Console are not connected.** Without Google Search Console you cannot see which searches find you. Without analytics you cannot tell which page or ad produced a lead. Both are free.
6. **No pricing guidance on the site.** Most homeowner searches include cost. A "what a design costs" section or a starting price would convert better than silence, but it is your call.
7. **Renders are renders.** The portfolio images are 3D renders, labeled as such. Mixing them with photos of finished work without labels would be misleading and hurts trust if someone notices.
8. **The old site had duplicate and screenshot images** (two portfolio slides were identical; three were phone screenshots showing a street address). Fixed in the new site.

## Plain-English glossary
- **SEO:** making the site easy for Google to understand and rank for what customers type.
- **Local SEO:** ranking in the map results and "near me" searches. Driven mostly by the Google Business Profile, reviews and consistent name, phone and email everywhere.
- **NAP:** name, address (or service area), phone. Must be identical across the site and every listing.
- **Schema / JSON-LD:** hidden labels in the page code that tell search engines "this is a business, this is its phone, these are its services."
- **robots.txt / sitemap.xml:** instructions and a page list that tell crawlers what to read.
- **llms.txt:** a short summary file written for AI assistants.
- **Canonical URL:** the one official address for a page, so duplicates do not split ranking.
- **Search Console:** Google's free dashboard showing which searches find your site.
- **Conversion rate:** the share of visitors who call or submit a form.
- **Speed-to-lead:** how fast you reply to a new lead. A fast reply wins more jobs.
- **Resend:** an email delivery service. It sends the new-lead alert and the automatic reply from your own domain.

**Narration:**

Two words on live TV helped open the Berlin Wall.
East Berlin. November 9th, 1989. Just before seven in the evening.
Günter Schabowski, spokesman for East Germany's ruling party, is wrapping up a long, dull press conference.
Shortly before, he'd been handed a note about new travel rules. He hadn't been in the meetings that wrote them.
Now he reads it aloud. Private trips abroad. Permanent exits. Through any border crossing.
A journalist calls out: when does it take effect?
Schabowski shuffles his papers. He squints.
Then he says it. "Sofort. Unverzüglich." Immediately, without delay.
But that wasn't the plan. The rules were meant to start the next day, with orderly visa applications.
Too late. West German TV aired the clip that night, and East Berliners were watching.
By late evening, thousands had crowded the checkpoint at Bornholmer Strasse.
The guards had no orders. Nobody wanted to use force.
At about 11:30, the officer in charge, Harald Jäger, opened the gate.
Other crossings followed.
Twenty-eight years of concrete and wire, opened in part by one line he hadn't read closely.
Two words. Immediately, without delay.
